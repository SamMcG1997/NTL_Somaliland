# Section 3: Literature Review

## 3.1 Introduction to NTL
Satellite-derived NTL data has become a widely used proxy for economic activity, especially in regions with limited or unreliable economic data. NTL data provides a consistent measure of human activity which can be applied across countries and time. A growing body of literature has demonstrated the link between light emissions and economic activity. The primary datasets used are the U.S. Defense Meteorological Satellite Program’s Operational Linescan System (DMSP-OLS), operational from 1992 to 2012, and the more recent Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) aboard the Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership (Suomi NPP) satellite, available from 2012 onward [^fn11].

Originally designed for meteorological purposes, the DMSP/OLS data has been repurposed by researchers to capture night time lights as a proxy for human settlement, infrastructure development, and economic intensity [@gibson2020nightlights]. One of the first usages of night time light data for scientific purposes was, Croft [-@croft1978nighttime] who used the data to discuss the size of gas flares from oil wells and their implications in terms of CO2 emissions and to detect squid fishing off of the coast of Japan [@croft1978nighttime]. This data was originally stored in an analog format. In 1992 the DMSP/OLS data became digitally available from 1992-2012. 

## 3.2 NTL in Economics
The first application of DMSP/OLS data on economic development appeared in 2002, with Sutton & Costanza where they estimated the market and non-market economic value of ecosystems using satellite data [@sutton2002nighttime]. Following this, studies began leveraging light emissions to understand the correlation between GDP and development. @henderson2012growth expanded the application of satellite data, combining it with national accounts to improve the measurement of economic growth, particularly in countries with weaker statistical systems. Their findings demonstrated that changes in light emissions can serve as a useful proxy for changes in GDP. 

In addition, NTL data has also been employed to assess the impacts of crises, such as natural disasters, conflict, and even corruption, by monitoring disruptions in lighting patterns [@akter2023wildfires; @hodler2014regional].

The core assumption underpinning the use of NTL to proxy for economic activity is that all else equal, more light is associated with more economic activity. As a society urbanises, industrialises, and expands infrastructure more electricity is used and more light is emitted at night. In essence, almost all consumption in the evenings requires additional lighting and this can be captured by satellites. However this relationship is dependent on economic structure, infrastructure and consumption patterns and can thus vary both across and within countries. (For a deeper discussion on the link between GDP and NTL please see @henderson2012growth.)

Despite the valuable contributions to economic research, the DMSP-OLS dataset frequently used has significant technical limitations including: coarse spatial resolution (approximately 2.7 km at nadir), sensor saturation in brightly lit urban areas (due to a 6-bit quantisation on board), and over-glow effects that blur city boundaries and inflate area estimates [@tuttle2013calibration; @abrahams2018overestimation] [^fn12]. This limits its accuracy for regional or local economic analyses and leads to overestimation of lit areas by up to 77% [@abrahams2018overestimation]. Economists have historically been slower than other fields to adopt VIIRS data, often relying on the longer DMSP time series [@gibson2020nightlights].

VIIRS data represents a notable advancement, offering finer spatial resolution (a 15-arc second spatial resolution with a granularity of approximately 742 meters), radiance measured in physical units, and improved correction algorithms addressing atmospheric interference and sensor noise [@wang2024blackmarble]. Importantly, VIIRS reduces saturation, enabling better differentiation of NTL in urban centers and detection of low-intensity lighting in rural areas [@elvidge2017viirs]. For instance, Gibson et al. [-@gibson2020nightlights] show that DMSP overestimates lit urban areas by 150% in Dar es Salaam and 5-fold in smaller towns like Bagamoyo compared to VIIRS. 

Nevertheless, VIIRS may still under-detect lights in sparsely populated regions or areas where light is intermittent, partly due to its satellite overpass occurring around 1:30 a.m. local time, when many rural households may have lights off [@gibson2019whichlights] [^fn13]. For example, there can be difficulties in estimating economic activities in countries with high shares of agricultural or pastoralist activity and may struggle to distinguish poor and densely populated areas from wealthy and sparsely populated areas [@perezsindin2021rural]. This is particularly relevant for contexts like Somaliland, where population density is low (approximately 22 persons/km²)[^fn14] and economic activity for the rural population is heavily reliant on pastoralism and agriculture, sectors that generate little night-time illumination. Therefore careful interpretation remains necessary. As detection thresholds and overpass times remain important caveats for any research. 

## 3.3 NTL Extensive & Intensive Margin
In economic research a differentiation is made between the extensive and intensive margin of NTL. The extensive margin is the change in the spatial extent of light. This means that areas which previously had no economic activity or undetectable economic activity (no detectable light emission) now emit light that can be measured by the satellite. This could be linked to rural electrification, urban expansion, and wider infrastructure development. 

The intensive margin captures changes in the radiance of already lit areas. Increases along the intensive margin may be due to changes in energy consumption, changes in types of lights used or higher levels of commercial and industrial activities. These changes increase the intensity of light in a given pixel.

Empirical evidence from countries such as India, Indonesia, and Papua New Guinea suggests that variation along the extensive margin is more strongly correlated with poverty reduction and subnational economic growth than changes in the intensive margin [@gibson2017intensive; @elizondo2023pacific]. Thus in analysing the relationship between NTL and GDP, the difference along those margins should be highlighted.

## 3.4 NTL & GDP
A key challenge in determining the relationship between NTL and GDP, is that elasticities vary across studies. There are three primary influences on these differences: firstly, and most importantly, the economic structure of the country or region in question; secondly, the satellite data source used (e.g. DMSP vs VIIRS); and finally, the spatial granularity of analysis. In particular, the relationship between light emissions and economic activity tends to be stronger in urbanised regions while weaker in rural or low-electrification settings [@gibson2024luminosity]. Similarly, VIIRS data, with its finer resolution, tends to yield more reliable estimates, especially in low-light rural contexts compared to earlier DMSP studies. 

The literature on NTL and GDP generally follows three broad approaches, cross-sectional estimations, longitudinal estimations, and applying previously estimated elasticities to data-scarce environments. 

Cross-sectional estimations tend to produce relatively large elasticities because structural differences are implicitly included in the NTL differences, such as urbanisation rates, economic structure, electrification, and policy differences. @gibson2022multisource, using county-level VIIRS data for China, estimate a cross-sectional elasticity of approximately 0.83 between NTL and GDP. Similarly, @gibson2021nightlights finds cross-sectional elasticities ranging from 0.69 to 1.65 across European regions depending on whether country fixed effects are included. 

Other papers use a longitudinal approach to explore the relationship between GDP and NTL over time. Longitudinal elasticities are often smaller than cross-sectional elasticities. @gibson2022multisource, for example, find that while the cross-sectional elasticity for Chinese counties is approximately 0.83, the longitudinal elasticity falls to only around 0.02 with a very small explanatory power. NTL data is generally better at explaining differences in economic activity across space than changes in economic activity over time [@chen2019viirs; @gibson2022multisource].

As mentioned, the spatial granularity of analysis has a strong impact on the size of the estimated elasticity. This is demonstrated in @gibson2024luminosity, where in Indonesia estimated longitudinal elasticities vary from approximately 0.02 at a district level to 0.16 at a province level. Variation is found in China, with county and district level elasticities estimated at 0.12 compared to 0.43 at province levels. The United States has an opposite relationship with elasticities for county level spatial units approximately 0.2 reducing to 0.05 for state level units. This demonstrates how important the spatial unit of measurement and the economic structure of countries is for applying elasticities to proxy for economic activity and exploring GDP and NTL relationships.

@henderson2012growth is one of the foundational and most influential studies relating NTL and GDP growth. Using DMSP data at a national level for 187 countries across 10 years they estimated an elasticity of approximately 0.3. Furthermore, @beyer2018districts estimated that, at a district level across 187 countries, a 1% increase in NTL emissions was associated with an increase in GDP of 0.27%, using both DMSP and VIIRS data from 1992-2016. However, they found substantial regional variation within South Asia, estimated elasticities were around 0.17 for Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and the Maldives, whilst Bhutan, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka had larger elasticities closer to 0.35. 

@beyer2022quarterly using just VIIRS data for 84 countries from 2013-2020, have estimated higher elasticities. When analysing quarterly GDP growth across emerging markets and developing economies they estimated that a 1% increase in GDP growth was associated with approximately a 1.55% increase in NTL emissions. Inverting this relationship implies an elasticity of 0.65% increase in GDP for every 1% increase in NTL emissions. 

Some studies suggest substantially larger elasticities in low-income countries. @hu2019illuminating, using both DMSP and VIIRS data, estimate a non-linear relationship between NTL and GDP per capita, with elasticities ranging from approximately 2.3 in low-income countries to near zero in high-income countries. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously. The estimated elasticities only become statistically significant when the poorest countries in the sample are excluded, reducing the suitability of these estimates for Somaliland. Furthermore, the authors note that background noise in VIIRS monthly data reduced statistical significance in low-light environments, leading them to rely more heavily on DMSP data for low-income estimations[^fn15].
