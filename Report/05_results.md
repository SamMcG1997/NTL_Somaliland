# Section 5: Somaliland's Economy from the Sky

## 5.1 National Trend
In 2012[^fn18] Somaliland recorded a total NTL radiance of approximately 1,724.6 nW/cm²/sr, with 569 pixels detected emitting light across the territory.  By 2023, this had grown to 10,788.8 nW/cm²/sr and 2,570 lit pixels, representing a sixfold increase in total light intensity and a fourfold expansion in the number of lit pixels. 

Following the links established in the literature review this hints at a period of sustained growth in economic activity, urban expansion, and electrification, as proxied by night-time luminosity. That is growth appears to be particularly accelerating after 2015 coinciding with investments into the Berbera Corridor and investments into electricity provision across Somaliland.

The table below indicates that both total radiance and the number of lit pixels accelerated particularly after 2016, suggesting an inflection point in Somaliland’s development. After 2016, growth accelerated reaching a peak of 38.3 percent in 2021 on the intensive margin, while growth on the extensive margin peaked in 2022 at 37.8 percent. This growth retracted significantly in 2023 with a growth of 10.9 and 5.9 percent on the intensive and extensive margin respectively. This is in line with key developments in Somaliland. Around 2015, key private sector initiatives and development cooperation projects in electricity provision started [@poplack2016powering]. Furthermore in 2016 the DP World Port deal was signed followed by key investment into the Berbera Port and Berbera Corridor infrastructure.

Increases in NTL after 2015 thus are likely to be driven by those port, road and energy infrastructure investments. However, the increases in energy investments might have led to the serving of pent up demand rather than increasing GDP. Nevertheless, this would only account for a small share of the increase in NTL and not significantly reduce the NTL-Economic Activity narrative. 

The number of lit pixels appears to be more volatile which is in line with research stating that the number of lit pixels are more responsive to changes in economic realities. Similar to radiance, the growth of the number of lit pixels was significantly higher after 2016 with growth rates of 16.7 and 18.4 percent in 2017 and 2018 respectively. Growth in the number of lit pixels decreased significantly in 2016 and 2019. This roughly coincides with the ban on imports of Somali Livestock in 2016 and Covid-19. Interestingly the nominal GDP figures increased in both 2016 and 2019

## 5.2 Regional Trends
The growth in both radiance and number of lit pixels hints at an increase in electrification and economic activity in Somaliland. However, the graphs below highlight high spatial inequality in this growth. Between 2012-2023 all regions experienced proportionally large growth in the radiance emitted. However, for most regions this growth only had a small effect in absolute terms, for example the total radiance of Sool has increased over twentyfold over the period, yet NTL emissions are still roughly only a tenth of Maroodi Jeex in 2012. It is further important to note that most of the growth in all regions occurred after the 2016 Berbera Port Deal, which was paired with significant investments into trade and energy infrastructure.

The graphs below show how the intensive and extensive margin of the regions have changed before and after the Berbera Port Deal. It is interesting to note that growth appears to accelerate in most regions after 2020. This is likely a consequence of key factors, such as the livestock-bans and delayed infrastructure development before 2020.

Maroodi Jeex houses Somaliland's capital Hargeisa, the largest city in Somaliland. It has attracted a significant amount of diaspora investment. Consequently, Hargeisa houses the largest share of formal business in the country [@mott2024q2]. This further led to the inflow of skilled workers, knowledge, development, and finance [@macgregor2008diaspora]. Furthermore, Maroodi Jeex includes important trade cities, such as Tog Wajale and Gabiley, which have significantly profited from the Berbera corridor. Furthermore, together with Awadal, the region has the most fertile ground for agriculture. 

The combination of key trade cities along the Berbera corridor, presence of the government and diaspora investment highlights the economic importance of the region. This importance reflected constant growth before 2016, which accelerated afterwards as villages along the Berbera Corridor profited from investments into the corridor and as trade through Hargeisa and Tog-Wajale increased their NTL output. This importance is reflected in the highest NTL on both the extensive and intensive margin in Somaliland, with Sahil reaching the 2012 levels of Maroodi Jeex in 2022. 

Sahil, due to the importance of the Berbera Port, is the second largest region in terms of NTL. Total NTL emissions in Sahil in 2012 were 244.1 nW/cm²/sr. This increased by almost ten-fold to 2371 by 2023. This increase was almost solely driven by Berbera, with NTL growth remaining stagnant before 2016, increasing after 2016 and accelerating after 2020 with the completion of the port expansion. In 2023, Berbera has the pixels with the highest NTL emissions in Somaliland. Berbera Port emissions alone account for 92.2% of emissions in 2023 in Sahil. The only growth outside of Berbera that is significant is along the Berbera corridor. Furthermore, it is important to note that during Q2 and Q3 of each year, many residents from Berbera move to the cooler regions, potentially impacting the yearly data on NTL used in the paper. 

Awdal, through Boroma (trade with Ethiopia), also benefits from the Berber Corridor, though on a much smaller extent than Maroodi Jeex and Sahil. Furthermore, the region benefits from its relatively fertile ground and thus has one of the highest rain-fed agricultural outputs [somaliland_fao2011]. The NTL may detect less economic growth than expected due to agricultural practices not being captured by the satellite imagery. Awdals NTL increased from 79.9 to 631.3 nW/cm²/sr, with this growth mostly concentrated in Booroma.

The concentration of NTL growth may have two key driving factors. First as of 2023, Boroma was the only major city properly connected to the Berbera corridor, decreasing trade friction and thus enabling growth in economic activity. Second, the rest of Awadal has faced significant economic challenges, including civil unrest. However, most importantly Awdal faced high cost of fuel and environmental degradation which significantly impacted local business and farmers [@acaps2025clanconflicts]. This has led to a significant amount of internal displacement, as people seeked income opportunities in Boroma. The Mixed Migration Centre estimates that 25% of the population in Boroma are displaced people [@4mi2026feasibility]. Potentially implying that the growth of NTL is driven by significant increases in the population and consumption. Evidence is insufficient to determine whether this will be a stable growth of economic activity, as there are limited accounts of new business creation outside of consumption related sectors.

Togdheer has seen a significant increase, but this increase is mostly driven by one city, Burco. This is largely due to Burco's importance as a key trade city (often called the second capital due to its historical relevance). Additionally, Togdheer has seen a significant influx of refugees caused by drought and conflict [@reliefweb2022togdheer]. The question is, to what extent these movements contribute to the increase in NTL without ensuring stable economic growth. That is, if the growth in NTL is mostly consumption driven and tied to livestock and remittances, then it can be expected that the situation remains volatile. Furthermore, in many camps streetlights are a rarity in IDP camps, while ACTED has installed solar panel streetlights it is unclear until when they are emitting detectable lights [@acted_somaliland_lighting]. 

Sanaag has the second lowest NTL emissions out of any region in Somaliland, with only Sool emitting less light. Sanaag emitted 12.4 nW/cm²/sr in 2012 which grew to 297.6 nW/cm²/sr by 2023. The region's dependency on pastoralism is most likely the reason for the limited NTL data recorded within the region. Interestingly, Sanaag is the region with the lowest proportion of NTL emissions coming from the region's major city. This is largely due to mining related settlements producing income in some villages. 

The pastoralist communities have been suffering from environmental degradation and increasing tensions within the regions leading to clashes throughout the years and in late 2024 [@hornobserver2025sanaag]. If the current trend continues it is unlikely to see a significant NTL growth that is connected to meaningful economic growth. However, there is an expectation that mining operations in Sanaag may expand which could result in an increase in NTL emissions through increased wealth in nearby villages.

Sool is the region of Somaliland with the lowest level of NTL emissions. From 11.8 nW/cm²/sr in 2012 to 167.8 nW/cm²/sr in 2023, Sool has consistently been the region with the lowest level of observable economic activity. In 2012, 100.0% of all detected light in the region was from Las Anod, this has decreased to 82.5% in 2023. The NTL increase in the region can be partially attributed to an electrification project by FCDO starting in 2018. However, conflict erupted in the region, causing NTL emissions to decrease after 2022. After the conflict Somaliland moved its main economic hub in the region to Ainabo. The city is profiting from the Khat (Miraa) trade that used to flow through Las Anod airport, but it is now transported by road through Ainabo. It is of interest to further study whether Ainabo will outgrow Las Anod in the coming years.

## 5.3 Urban Trends
NTL growth in Somaliland is further characterised by the differences in NTL intensity between rural-urban areas, in addition to the high spatial inequality. Analysis of NTL data that is highlighted by the maps above, shows that radiance remains clustered in urban areas. The proportion of the population who live in urban areas was last calculated at 53% in 2014 using the Somaliland Population Estimation Survey [@somaliland2024figures]. It is likely that the percentage living in urban areas is much higher now following droughts in the past decade.

In 2012, three cities, Hargeisa, Berbera, and Burco, accounted for 92.7% of all NTL emissions in Somaliland: Hargeisa alone contributed 69.2%, Berbera 14.1%, and Burco 9.4%. This is in line with the economic realities during this time. Hargeisa in 2012 was the most populated city in Somaliland, housing the government, a comparatively large retail, hospitality and service sector and serving as a key trading point. Berbera in 2012 already was a key export point for livestock, though at a smaller scale before DP World investments in 2016. Burco, was considered the second-most populated city in Somaliland [@radio_somaliland_burao] and holds importance as a central trading hub (Burco Meat & Produce market). 

By 2023, the share of NTL emissions from these three cities had declined slightly to 80.5% Hargeisa accounted for 52.6%, Berbera for 20.2% and Burco for 7.7%. This diffusion can be partially attributed to the impact of the Berbera Corridor on smaller villages along the Corridor. However large parts of the country remain unlit, despite the presence of many villages and communities. Thus, while the reduction suggests growth of economic activity in rural areas, the urban dominance remains stark.

As evident by the comparative maps above, previously unlit villages along the corridor have seemed to catch up significantly with the regional capitals of Erigavo and Las Anod indicating the increasing divide in economic realities between cities along and cities outside the Berbera corridor. It is however, also important to note, that growth especially in Sool has been dampened by the conflict in the region.

As shown in the table below the Urban-Rural divide is most significant in Awdal, where Boroma holds the highest share of NTL emittance compared to its region. In 2012, 100% of the NTL detected was in Boroma at 70 nW/cm²/sr. By 2023, this had only decreased to 94.8% of the emissions. This is partially due to the relatively high share of farmers emitting less NTL and significant population movement to Boroma, due to droughts and the search for economic opportunities. Similarly, in Maroodi Jeex, Hargeisa’s share of regional emissions fell only slightly, from 98.4% in 2012 to 89.3% in 2023.

It is evident that the main driver of NTL in Togdheer is Burco which hosts one of the most important trading points for livestock within the region. The importance of this city poses the question to what extent the pastoralists activities are captured in NTL. That is, while their daily herding lives are not captured by NTL, their influence on the Somaliland economy could be captured by the key trading points, as in Burco. 

Other regional capitals, such as Erigavo (Sanaag) and Las Anod (Sool), remain comparatively dim. Both serve as small trade hubs with existing airports. Both cities are key trading points with Somalia and have deep linkages with some of the Somalia regions. However, since the conflict, Las Anod has to some extent lost this status, with trade now flowing through Ainabo instead of Las Anod. Furthermore Erigavo houses a large share of the mining activities in Somaliland. While potentially important for the future economic development, it has not yet seen significant growth.

Over the past decade, drought and employment opportunities have pushed many pastoralists and villagers to relocate to cities, particularly Hargeisa. It is clear to see that NTL growth remains concentrated. It is likely that many areas have a very similar standard of life as in 2012. The largest towns in Sanaag and Sool emit less light than small villages in the wealthier regions. Recurring draughts and limited policies to bolster economic activity in these regions hint at a potentially widening gap. The combination of these stressors could increase existing tensions and increase the potential for relapse into conflict in the regions.
