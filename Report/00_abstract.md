# Abstract
Somaliland, unrecognised internationally[^fn1], has remained relatively stable since declaring independence in 1991. Somaliland is largely unable to access international funding and thus investments have been driven by the national budget and private investment. However, Somaliland’s government spending is constrained to a yearly budget of around $258m USD (2024), which is mostly spent on salaries. Thus there has been limited fiscal space for government investment in economic development, with development sourced primarily from the private sector, diaspora, and deals with foreign companies such as DP World.

Somaliland’s economy is largely informal with most formal businesses operating in retail, logistics or hospitality. These sectors are dependent on trade with Somaliland's few trade partners, especially Ethiopia, and stable household income. Household income depends mostly on pastoralism and remittances. The high reliance on income from Pastoralism (Hajj), Ethiopian trade, and remittances exposes Somaliland to income shocks from drought, livestock import bans, weakening remittances, and disruptions to global trade. This has been evident when economic growth contracted when Hajj-livestock exports were weak.

Currently, scarcity of high-quality data leads to lack of reliable evidence on Somaliland's economic trajectory. The GDP figures are subject to uncertainty and there are no available subnational GDP estimations, limiting the ability to develop evidence-based policies. This paper aims to fill this gap using Night-Time Lights to explore economic activity in Somaliland from 2013-2023. The analysis focuses on the intensive (light intensity) and extensive (number of lit pixels) margin of NTL-data, to examine regional disparities and the overall change in economic activity.

This paper, applying elasticities from other studies, finds that at the lower bound, NTL converted real GDP is \$3,351m in 2023 compared to the official statistics of \$2,497m USD. With an upper bound of \$6,474m. This indicates that official figures underestimate Somaliland's real GDP in 2023. However, caution in interpretation is warranted, as similar research points out NTLs weakness in the connection of NTL to GDP or consumption growth.

The growth in NTL specifically occurs after increased investments into the Berbera Corridor, increased access to electricity and the Berbera Port deal after 2015. This indicates the importance of infrastructure investments in Somaliland's economic development. However, this growth story has exacerbated regional divides in economic activities, with growth mostly concentrated along the Berbera Corridor. While most NTL activity remains in urban hubs, the Berbera corridor has also led to increases in NTL in villages along the corridor. It is important to note that changes in certain economic activities, specifically pastoralism, have likely not been captured by this study. Further, studies could study the exact impact of the Berbera Corridor or integrate other alternative data to capture pastoralist activity and provide a more in-depth account of Somaliland's development. 

[^fn1]: Israel became the first country to recognise Somaliland on the 26th of December 2025.
