# Section 1: Introduction 

Somaliland is a self-declared sovereign state in the Horn of Africa that broke away from Somalia in 1991, after a civil war which left the region largely destroyed, including up to 90% of the capital city Hargeisa [@icg2006somaliland]. Since then there have been multiple peaceful democratic transitions of power and Hargeisa has been largely rebuilt and is home to an estimated 1.2 million people, roughly 20% of Somaliland's total population [@worldpopulationreview_hargeysa]. 

Somaliland’s government's revenue is dependent on trade, especially livestock exports, and household consumption related tax. Inland revenue is largely constrained to a few cities. Thus, the government revenue remains small and a significant share is spent on salary, especially for military and police personnel [@slmof2024fiscal]. The limited fiscal space constrains the government's ability to invest, with spending largely focused on the Berbera Port, the Berbera Corridor, and water and energy infrastructure.

This has led to a weak business environment, as businesses have limited access to paved roads, energy, water, finance and skilled labour. In addition, businesses face high informal competition with 77% of employment estimated to be informal in Hargeisa [@somaliland_msme2019]. That is why, the economy largely continues to rely on income from remittances, livestock and business income within the logistics, retail and hospitality sectors. Furthermore ,the weak business environment has not been able to absorb the urbanisation trend in Somaliland leaving approximately 70 percent of those under 30 unemployed [@obsie2025dhs].

The low diversification of household income and government revenue have left the economy exposed to shocks through global trade disruptions, droughts and weakening remittances. In the past, disruptions to livestock trade have proven to be especially detrimental to Somaliland's economy, reducing household income and government revenue.

Responses to the challenges are limited by weak national statistics, which have been insufficiently funded. This limited funding has led to complications in Somaliland's Gross Domestic Product (GDP) estimates, with out of date assessment of the Somaliland population, poor measures of economic activity, and trade statistics which vary drastically both yearly and between government ministries. The limited data coverage and systemic measurement biases weakens the government ability to design evidence based policies. 

To address this limitation, this paper uses Nighttime Light satellite data, to examine the spatial variation and concentration of nighttime light (NTL) emissions across time from 2013 to 2023. Providing the first systematic and descriptive analysis of NTL data in Somaliland. This study aims to offer new insights into both national and subnational economic dynamics. In addition this paper aims at continuing to demonstrate how satellite-based data can be used as an innovative tool in regions with limited formal data. Laying the groundwork for future case-based evaluations of interventions in Somaliland, such as the economic impacts of drought-induced displacement or large-scale infrastructure investments. 

The use of NTL has seen a significant increase in estimating economic indicators globally. This rich set of literature ranges from employing NTL directly to estimating GDP or economic activity more broadly. However, recent studies have highlighted a key drawback in the utilisation of NTL on economic activity. Therefore results should be interpreted with caution. The literature especially points out that the Defense Meteorological Satellite Program’s Operational Linescan System (DMSP-OLS) has significant drawbacks in its use as a proxy for economic activity and therefore this paper utilises Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) data ranging from 2012 to 2023.

The following sections provide an overview of Somaliland's economy (section 2) and the current literature on the use of NTL in economics (section 3). Section 4 elaborates on the methodology and limitations when using NTL data. Sections 5 and 6 examine Somaliland’s NTL emissions, its implications for spatial inequality, and explores new GDP estimates. Finally, section 7 offers a discussion on the results.
