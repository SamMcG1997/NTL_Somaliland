# Section 4: Methodology

## 4.1 Nighttime Lights
Night-time lights data provide a proxy measure of spatial economic activity through observation of artificial lighting coming from, electric lights, infrastructure, and industrial activity. To explore economic activity we aggregate radiance intensity across defined administrative and urban boundaries.

There are four products produced by Black Marble, VNP46A1-VNP46A4. This paper uses VNP46A4 (Annual NTL data) for analysis. This is to maximise the robustness of the data and to compare the change in NTL-levels across longer time-horizons and to mitigate the risk of seasonal variation. The data begins in April 2012 and continues until 2023. The DNB sensor records upward-emitted and reflected visible light at night, measured as radiance in nanoWatts per square centimetre per steradian (nW/cm²/sr).

As discussed in the literature review section, this paper will be analysing the extensive and intensive margin of NTL change. The extensive margin represents the number of pixels that record a radiance value greater than 0, in other words showing detectable light. The intensive margin measures the total radiance of light in the pixels in the regions in question. NTL measurements were aggregated across three different geographic granularities at, national, regional, and urban levels.

Administrative boundaries for Somaliland were acquired from a public data set published by the Sababi Institute, a Somaliland-based research organization.[^fn16] Initial inspection of the shapefile found that the port area of Berbera, which represents one of the most economically significant and brightly illuminated locations in Somaliland, was partially excluded from existing boundary definitions. Exclusion of this area would systematically understate national and regional radiance totals and bias estimates of economic activity.

To address this limitation, the Somaliland shapefile was manually extended to fully include the Berbera port area.[^fn17] The modification was implemented using GIS tools to ensure continuity with existing administrative boundaries while incorporating all visible illuminated infrastructure associated with the port and surrounding urban development. The adjusted boundary was subsequently used for all national-level NTL aggregation.

We conducted quality assurance with different shapefiles to ensure that the usage of Sababi-derived shapefiles did not systemically change our results. Supplementary code is available in the GitHub repository which demonstrates the minor differences between using the Sababi Shapefile with the Berbera addition, the shapefile from the Global Administrative Areas database (GADM), and the GADM shapefile with all borders extended by approximately 300m as an alternative method to capture emissions from Berbera. 

To examine sub-national patterns of development, night-time lights were aggregated across Somaliland’s six administrative regions: Maroodi Jeex, Togdheer, Awdal, Sahil, Sanaag, and Sool. Regional shapefiles were derived from the adjusted national boundary dataset.

For each region, NTL measures were constructed by masking the raster dataset using regional polygons and aggregating radiance values across all pixels contained within each administrative boundary. This approach allows direct comparison of economic activity levels across regions while preserving spatial heterogeneity.

Shapefiles defining urban extents are not currently available for most Somaliland cities. To overcome this limitation, bounding boxes were manually constructed around six major urban centres using [GeoJSON.io](https://geojson.io/#map=2/0/20). These cities were selected based on population size, economic importance, and observed illumination patterns within the NTL dataset. Visualisations of the NTL emissions of the cities in question can be seen in Annex B. 

The use of bounding boxes introduces potential measurement error because these polygons may include surrounding non-urban land. However, this limitation is partially mitigated by the spatial structure of Somaliland’s settlements. With the exception of Hargeisa, most urban centres are surrounded by sparsely populated or unlit areas. As a result, the inclusion of surrounding land contributes minimal additional radiance and is unlikely to materially bias estimates of total urban light emissions.

Bounding boxes were deliberately widely defined to ensure that all illuminated urban infrastructure was captured across the full study period, including potential urban expansion. This approach prioritises temporal consistency and reduces the risk of underestimating economic growth driven by outward urban development.

This paper uses annual data, as many pixels in the monthly data or daily data are of poor quality or gap filled due to insufficient observations. The overall quality of the annual data is high, our robustness checks found that on average across all years 99.9% of the data points were of good quality. The year with the lowest quality data was in 2017, where 431 of the 804,375 pixels were of poor quality, and 10 pixels were gap-filled, in other words 99.95% of data points were of good quality. Across each year, the area with poor quality pixels has been focussed on the Eerigavo mountains (the tallest part of Somaliland) where there is little to no artificial light emitted. The VNP46A4 data provides pre-processed data which has been corrected for stray light, clouds, and gap-fill areas with insufficient data points. 

## 4.2 Application to GDP
This paper uses elasticities that have been derived from other studies on NTL and GDP to provide a wide estimation of the potential real GDP growth in Somaliland across the past decade. The studies often use log linear regressions to derive the NTL and GDP relationship,such as:

$$\ln(\text{GDP}_{it}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(\text{NTL}_{it}) + \varepsilon_{it}$$

Where *GDP*<sub>it</sub> represents economic activity for region or country, *i*, at time *t*, and *NTL*<sub>it</sub> represents the observed night-time light emissions. The coefficient $\beta$ captures the elasticity of economic activity with respect to NTL. The elasticity of NTL and GDP estimates the percentage change in economic activity from a one percent change in night time light intensity. 

No single elasticity estimate can be assumed to apply directly to Somaliland. Instead, this paper uses a range of estimates from the literature to bound the possible relationship between NTL and GDP. We have elected to use global estimates and studies containing low and middle income countries, recognising that whilst they may not be perfect estimations they will better approximate the economic structure in Somaliland than countries like China, Europe, and the US amongst others. (Please see Annex C for a table detailing other examples of elasticities in the literature). 

The lower bound is 0.3, based on @henderson2012growth which is widely cited and used as a conservative estimate of the relationship between NTL and economic activity. Including papers such as @chodorowreich2018cash, providing additional robustness in using 0.3 as a lower bound. 

For an upper estimate of 0.65 the results of @beyer2022quarterly are used. The authors estimated that a 1 percent increase in GDP resulted in a 1.55 percent increase in NTL emissions. We have inverted this to approximate the elasticity of NTL changes on GDP, deriving an elasticity of 0.65% increase in GDP for every 1% increase in NTL. The authors argue that the relationship remains relatively stable across country groups and this elasticity was applied by @mcsharry2024southsudan in the data-constrained environment of South Sudan where GDP estimates are unavailable. 

These elasticities, (0.3 and 0.65), provide a range to estimate how changes in NTL and GDP have occurred over the past decade. Using a range instead of a single-point estimate highlights the inherent uncertainty with this approach and allows a more cautious interpretation of the analysis. The realism of these elasticity estimates will be further elaborated on in the results section.

It is important to emphasise that the resulting GDP estimates should not be interpreted as direct measurements of economic output. Instead, they represent inferred estimates of economic growth conditional on elasticities estimated in other contexts. The wide range of elasticities deliberately reflects both uncertainty in the underlying satellite-GDP relationship and structural characteristics of Somaliland’s economy, including the prevalence of informal economic activity and uneven electrification patterns.

One important caveat is the assumption that the elasticities remain constant throughout the period 2012-2023. If NTL becomes less related to GDP growth across this period then the estimates of true GDP growth may be biased. One factor which may influence the elasticity is if the composition of the economy meaningfully changes, with less detectable economic activities becoming more or less prevalent. However, the elasticities have been derived from papers exploring the impact of NTL on GDP across long horizons, @hu2019illuminating use DMSP data from 1992-2013, @beyer2022quarterly use VIIRS data from 2012-2020, and @beyer2018districts use DMSP data from 1992-2013 and VIIRS data from 2012-2017. This provides some validity that the elasticities have already included any structural impacts. 

## 4.3 Limitations
There are some important limitations in using NTL to estimate economic activity in Somaliland. Somaliland has a large informal economy, which emits less light as a proportion of GDP it generates and thus can weaken the connection between NTL and economic activity. Furthermore, a large share of economic activity in Somaliland is driven by pastoralists who are unlikely to emit significant amounts of NTL. Based on this, NTL alone may understate economic activity in Somaliland. In addition, NTL might be driven by pent-up demand for electricity and extension of power grids rather than increases in economic activity.

Similarly, while late overpass can lead to higher spatial resolution it also can lead to a decreased ability to approximate economic activity. VIIRs observes luminosity at around 1:30 am (UTC). This is often after economic activities reduce for the night, for many countries. Thus, increased economic activity, if they happen early during the day, may not be reflected in an increase in NTL data [@gibson2024luminosity]. These changes in consumption may be particularly underestimated if there is an increase in white LEDs being used [@roman2018blackmarble]. That is, VIIRs suite is unreliable in detecting blue-light emission peaks of white LEDs, potentially underestimating the true radiance per pixel.

The detection threshold of NTL may introduce further bias, densely populated areas tend to generate stronger detectable signals, whilst sparsely populated regions may remain undetected even when economic activity is present. This is particularly relevant in places where population density is low and employment is often informal, this may result in too much emphasis being placed on urban areas as economic centres and underestimating economic activity in rural areas. 

There have been significant demographic shifts in Somaliland, with many families becoming internally displaced people (IDPs), in 2023 the Somaliland Government estimated approximately 380,000 [@somaliland2024figures]. This is a significant proportion of the population regardless of which population estimation is used. 

IDPs are often triggered by worsening climate conditions and their loss in livestock, but reduction in pastoralist activity may not be fully captured by NTL. At the same time clan safety nets may allow IDPs to contribute to consumption activities in the city potentially increasing nightlight. This consumption led increase in NTL, may not sufficiently capture economic damages from loss of pastoralist activities.

These limitations warrant a careful interpretation of the GDP translations and narratives of changes in household well-being. It is important to note that whilst NTL can provide a proxy for economic activity, it will be underestimating informal, pastoral and agriculture activities. This holds especially true to losses in economic activity of pastoralists and agriculture due to climate change. Thus translating NTL into GDP growth in Somaliland may overestimate economic growth. Nevertheless, NTL data can act as a useful tool to analyse changes in economic activity in the absence of official data. Thus, the analysis provides useful insights to changes in economic activity in Somaliland, but the results should be interpreted with caution.
