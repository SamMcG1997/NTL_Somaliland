# Section 2: Somaliland Overview

Somaliland was a British protectorate, which became independent on June 26th 1960, five days later Somaliland entered a union with Somalia. The complicated situation of clan dynamics and oppression led to a civil war between the Somaliland National Movement and Somali forces and the Isaac genocide. Following the civil war, Somaliland emerged as an independent country in 1991. In 2001, Somaliland voted on their constitution and there have been democratic transitions of power ever since. Somaliland has remained relatively peaceful since independence and its estimated nominal GDP has grown from \$1.8bn USD in 2012 to \$4.0bn USD in 2023 [@somaliland2023gdp]. This has been partially attributed to the existence of a strong private sector and clan elder influence on the government. However, real GDP per capita decreased from \$584 in 2012 to \$547 in 2023 and international support remains limited as Somaliland remains largely unrecognised. 

Somaliland has historically been a pastoralist economy and livestock remains an integral part of both Somali culture and the economy. The significance of livestock for Somaliland's economy is reflected in the country's trade statistics with livestock on average accounting for 71% of exports across the past 5 years[^fn2]. However, this dependence on livestock exposes the economy to adverse supply- and demand-side shocks, through droughts, livestock diseases and recently the COVID-19 pandemic. Environmental degradation has led to loss of livestock leading to increasing displacement of pastoralists. Saudi Arabia’s ban on Somaliland livestock from 2016–2018 due to Rift Valley fever, along with COVID-19-related Hajj restrictions, led to export losses estimated at \$777 million and \$42 million respectively [@mtimet2021livestock]. 

Internal displacement and limited income opportunities in rural areas has led to high levels of urbanisation in Somaliland. Somaliland's urban and rural population is relatively young, with 48% of household members being under the age of 15 [@unfpa2020slhds]. However, only 43% of male household members and 50% of female household members had attended primary school, hindering human capital development and the potential for a positive demographic dividend. While years of schooling have increased in the past years, overall quality of education remains largely stagnant. This constrains productivity, which poses a challenge for Somaliland's business environment, as businesses have limited access to skilled labour.

The business environment is further strained by significant competition with informal businesses. Currently, Somaliland's private sector is largely informal and consists of micro and small businesses, with 77% of employment estimated to be informal in Hargeisa [@somaliland_msme2019]. Furthermore, the formal sector remains highly concentrated in Hargeisa, with 377 new business registrations in Q2 2024, while Berbera and Boorama recorded only 12 and 11 respectively [@mott2024q2]. The formal business activity is dominated by the trade logistics, retail and hospitality sectors, highlighting Somaliland's dependency on stable trade and household consumption. It is important to note that household consumption is dependent on remittances and livestock trade. Thus a shock to either could have significant ripple effects on Somaliland's economy. 

The government's ability to fund policies and projects that boost diversification are limited by fiscal constraints. In 2024 the government budget was around \$258 million USD[^fn3], with approximately 57 percent allocated to salaries for civil servants, police, and the military [@slmof2024fiscal]. This leaves little available funding for infrastructure projects or public services. Thus the government is dependent on private and foreign direct investments which is limited, due to Somaliland unrecognised status.

A potential driver of business diversification could be the Berbera Port. The Berbera Port has historical significance for Somaliland's economy as it is one of the government's major revenue sources with 65% of import taxes in 2024 collected there [@slmof2025q2; @stepputat2019berbera]. In 2016 the government of Somaliland signed a deal with DP World to expand the port and improve the operations of the port [@dpworld_terminal]. DP World agreed to invest \$442m in the development of better port infrastructure [@ruis2022afcfta]. Alongside this, the UAE and the UK contributed to the development of the Berbera Corridor, which is a 225km road connecting Berbera  to Wajale, the main border city with Ethiopia, passing through Hargeisa.

Berbera Port and the Corridor are the largest infrastructure projects in Somaliland and could drive significant economic growth. The corridor facilitates trade flows from Ethiopia and improves accessible infrastructure reducing barriers for business. However, limited access to finance, low educational attainment and absence of formal recognition remain as significant barriers. Furthermore, the benefits may be spatially constrained with limited spillovers. Consequently, Somalilanders continue to rely on pastoralism and sustenance farming for their livelihoods. 

Furthermore, households in Somaliland rely heavily on remittances. Somaliland is one of the most remittance reliant countries in the world [@worldbank2016somaliland], with inflow remittances often exceeding \$1bn a year. However, remittances are often paid through remittance companies and exports are also often paid through these same companies, thereby making it difficult to assess the true “remittance” rate. A 2012 survey estimated that approximately 40% of households receive remittances, averaging \$271 USD per month [@fsnau2013familyties]. However, this is likely biased since the rural areas selected had a money transfer agent nearby, and are thus more likely to receive remittances than less connected rural areas. Furthermore, despite being 58% of the sample, urban households received 87% of remittances highlighting the inequality of access. However, urban households likely transfer some of the remittances to their extended family in rural areas.

Barriers to doing business and dependence on sustenance farming, pastoralism and remittances limit economic growth and expose the country to both internal and external shocks. Furthermore, whilst the Berbera Corridor may increase income of households it could also increase inequality leading to more clan conflicts. 

## Somaliland's GDP

GDP per capita acts as an effective and easy to calculate proxy for living standards, income, and economic activity (Please see @bergh2009gdp for a more complete discussion.). Improving GDP growth rate is often a government’s top economic priority, and therefore estimations of GDP are of vital importance to policy makers [collectivewellbeing2023gdp][^fn4].

There is a wide array of literature which discusses the shortcomings of using GDP as a proxy for living standards [@giannetti_gdp_limits][^fn5]. This includes, limited accounting for social cost, limited distinction between survival and status goods, wellbeing, income distribution, environmental externalities and the informal economy. In lower-income countries, GDP measurements can influence aid eligibility and how development assistance funding may be allocated [@collectivewellbeing2023gdp]. This can lead to perverse incentives to influence the GDP figures of a country. 

Furthermore, GDP estimates often rely on household surveys, enterprise censuses, and sectoral accounts which are often infrequent or incomplete. Lack of capacity and standardised methods leaves room for political stakeholders to adopt measures that suit their own political goals [@jerven2013poornumbers]. This can especially hold true where data is connected to allocation and decision making, where countries are fragmented among ethnic, clan or other identity lines. In these cases, data can be misrepresented to sustain certain narratives.

The Somaliland Ministry of Planning and National Development publishes yearly GDP estimates and includes a short description of the methodology used[^fn6]. Across the 9 years from 2015-2023 (inclusive) nominal GDP per capita has increased by an estimated 38.7%, approximately 4.3% a year. However, much of this increase was driven by the 20.2% difference between 2018-2019. This may be due to the last household survey which was conducted in 2018. The household survey component of GDP has not been updated since 2018, if there is the same scale of difference as the last time then GDP per capita could be significantly higher[^fn7]. 

Despite nominal GDP per capita growing by 38.7% across the 9 years, real GDP per capita (using 2017 as a base year), has actually shrunk by 6.5%. This highlights the key issue of import driven inflation which has consistently increased the cost of living in the country. The GDP figures imply that living standards in real terms have decreased since 2015, however, this is reliant upon the assumptions that the GDP data is accurate.

Like many low income countries, Somaliland has low statistical capacity, leading to limitations in the calculation of Somaliland's GDP [@angrist2021growth]. One of the main issues facing the creation of accurate GDP and especially GDP per capita statistics in Somaliland is that the population estimates are based off of a survey conducted in 2014 by the UNFPA across the whole of Greater Somalia[^fn8]. These population estimates have then been uprated by 2.8% a year since[^fn9]. Using a survey 10 years out of date for population estimates may be resulting in biased estimates. Especially when considering that repeated droughts, instability in South-Central Somalia, and migration from Ethiopia are likely to have affected the population statistics. Following, is a summary of additional methodological concerns.

**Household Final Consumption.** Somaliland’s Central Statistics Department utilises an expenditure survey for calculating the consumption of households, the last iteration of the household survey was in March 2017. The expenditure data is then multiplied by population estimates. The accuracy of the population estimates is highly uncertain. This is then adjusted by a factor related to import levels, which, as discussed below, is another suspect data source. 

Therefore, the accuracy of the final consumption levels are uncertain. During the past years the country has been impacted by a livestock ban, Covid-19 and several droughts. However, consumption levels have only decreased during Covid. This could potentially be explained by remittances offsetting the impact of the export ban and the drought mostly impacting pastoralists who make up a marginal fraction of the final consumption within Somaliland. However, some impact from these other factors is expected. 

**Net Exports.** Exports and imports are partially retrieved from data collected from the customs authority and an estimation from the UN Comtrade database. The trade statistics produced by Ministries in the Government of Somaliland are highly variable, with staple goods facing yearly changes in amounts imported in excess of 300%, implying errors in data collections and rendering conclusions drawn from the data unreliable [@somaliland_trade2021][^fn10].

As the Comtrade database does not consider Somaliland as its own country, the data from Comtrade is approximated from the Somalia data, by assuming that the trade share is roughly equal to Somaliland's share of the population of Somalia, roughly one third.. Again using 2014 population estimates is likely to be introducing additional biases to the data. In addition the assumption that the trade share is roughly equal to the share of population has not been tested, as trade volume is also influenced by factors such as resource endowments, infrastructure, and access to international markets. Therefore the approximation from the Comtrade data might further bias the GDP estimate of the country.

**Government Final Consumption.** While the household consumption relies on projections from past data, the government final consumption data is relatively reliable as it is taken from the state's fiscal revenue and expenditure data. 

**Gross Fixed Capital Formation (GFC).** The gross fixed capital formation, otherwise termed investment, is calculated using import data from the customs authority including imports of building materials, machinery and transport equipment. As discussed, the import data is highly variable implying that there are likely to be substantial errors in GFC calculations too.

**A further complication** is the large size of the informal economy in many developing contexts. Informal sector activity is often excluded or undercounted in official statistics due to its nature of being undocumented [@angrist2021growth]. In many low-income countries this accounts for a significant share of total output and employment and therefore their omission or mismeasurement leads to systematic underestimation of GDP [@angrist2021growth]. This issue is particularly relevant in post-conflict and fragile states where informal markets may dominate the economy, such as in Somaliland, which when coupled with the absence of strong state institutions, and a limited ability for statistical agencies to collect robust data, leads to significant biases.

It is likely that the GDP estimates from the government of Somaliland does not reflect the reality on the ground. The direction of bias depends on the nature of inaccuracy in the data such as whether population projection over- or understates the population or whether trade statistics and household consumption are over- or understated.

The uncertainty surrounding the GDP data of Somaliland demonstrates the difficulty of using it for any analysis or policy making. Furthermore, since the GDP figures at a national level are uncertain, any attempt to examine sub-national trends is merely impossible. However, nighttime light data allows for a less biased overview of the country. There are several methodological difficulties with this approach but it will ensure that there is a consistent approach to examining how economic activity (as proxied by night time lights) has evolved since 2012.
