# Section 7: Discussion

Somaliland has developed a largely stable democratic system, although tensions and conflict among clan and sub-clan groups remain. As an unrecognised state, Somaliland receives limited foreign direct investment, has restricted access to international aid and has a limited national budget. At the same time, statistical capacity remains limited, making it difficult to accurately measure economic activity and economic growth. This paper utilised NTL data as an alternative source of evidence, providing insights into the nature and spatial distribution of economic development across Somaliland.

Between 2012 and 2023, Somaliland experienced a range of shocks, including droughts, internal conflicts, Covid-19, and livestock import bans by Saudi Arabia. Livestock exports in 2023 remained below their 2015 levels, placing pressure on household incomes and government revenues. Nevertheless, despite these challenges, NTL data indicate substantial growth on both the extensive and intensive margins over the study period.

The most important result provides evidence that economic growth has been highly uneven across Somaliland. This is the first time that a comparative assessment of economic activity across Somaliland has been undertaken. Regional and urban analysis indicate that a substantial share of NTL growth has been concentrated in areas connected to the Berbera Corridor. Growth in Berbera and Hargeisa has been particularly pronounced, while many areas outside the corridor have experienced considerably slower increases in NTL. The acceleration in NTL growth after 2016 coincides with major investments in port, energy, and transport infrastructure associated with the Berbera Corridor, suggesting that these developments may have contributed to increased economic activity in connected regions.

This pattern is consistent with Somaliland's broader economic structure. Business activity remains concentrated in a relatively narrow range of sectors, including pastoralism, agriculture, trade and logistics, retail, and hospitality. The trade, logistics, retail, and hospitality sectors appear to have benefited most directly from growing trade flows through Berbera. In contrast, pastoralism and agriculture continue to face significant challenges related to environmental degradation, drought, livestock import restrictions, and limited access to infrastructure and electricity.

The findings point towards two distinct economic realities within Somaliland. On one hand, regions connected to the Berbera Corridor have experienced substantial growth in both the brightness and spatial extent of night-time lights. On the other hand, growth has been considerably weaker in many peripheral regions, particularly Sool, Sanaag, and parts of northern Awdal. Limited electrification, poor infrastructure, fewer employment opportunities, lower levels of investment, and continued instability may all contribute to this pattern.

However, these findings should be interpreted carefully. NTL data is likely to understate economic activity in pastoral and agricultural areas, where production generates relatively little detectable light. This limitation is particularly relevant for regions such as Sool and Sanaag, where pastoralism remains an important component of livelihoods. Nevertheless, these regions have also experienced droughts, internal displacement, livestock losses, and insecurity during the study period, factors which may have constrained economic activity and contributed to slower growth in observed NTL.

The analysis also reveals a clear urban-rural divide. Berbera, Burco, and Hargeisa account for a large share of Somaliland's total NTL emissions, while many rural areas remain only weakly illuminated. Although villages located along the Berbera Corridor have experienced some increases in NTL, there is limited evidence of broader spillovers into more remote areas. This suggests that economic growth may be becoming increasingly concentrated in a small number of urban centres.

A further finding is the substantial divergence between official GDP statistics and GDP estimates derived from NTL growth. Across the range of elasticities examined in this paper, NTL-based estimates imply substantially faster economic growth than official statistics suggest. While these results should not be interpreted as direct measurements of GDP, they raise important questions regarding the accuracy of existing economic statistics and highlight the potential value of incorporating alternative data sources into economic measurement exercises.

Given these limitations, NTL data should be viewed as a complement rather than a substitute for traditional economic statistics. Future efforts to improve measurement of economic activity in Somaliland would benefit from combining NTL data with household surveys, administrative data, trade statistics, and field-based studies.

Looking forward, Somaliland continues to face significant structural challenges. The economy remains heavily dependent on remittances, livestock exports, and the consumption of imported goods. As a result, shocks affecting remittance inflows, livestock production, or export markets can have economy-wide consequences by reducing household incomes and consumption.
Diversifying the economy is a key policy objective. However, the government's ability to support development is constrained by limited fiscal resources. While the Berbera Corridor appears to have supported growth in connected villages and cities, many areas outside the corridor continue to face substantial development challenges.
The persistence of uneven development raises important policy concerns. Many regions outside the corridor have experienced only limited increases in NTL over the period, suggesting that economic opportunities remain concentrated geographically. Policymakers may therefore wish to consider strategies that promote growth in lagging regions, particularly Sool, Sanaag, and Awdal. Expanding economic opportunities in these regions could broaden the benefits of growth while helping to reduce regional disparities and associated sources of instability.
Overall, the findings highlight both the opportunities and challenges facing Somaliland's development trajectory. The evidence suggests that infrastructure investment and growing trade activity have been associated with substantial increases in economic activity, particularly along the Berbera Corridor. At the same time, the spatial concentration of this growth raises important questions regarding regional inequality and the long-term inclusiveness of Somaliland's development model.
